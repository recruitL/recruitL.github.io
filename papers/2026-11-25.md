<summary>英文摘要</summary>
<p>Understanding the excitation of black hole quasinormal modes is essential for interpreting gravitational-wave signals and inferring black hole properties from them. In this work, we investigate the excitation of the quasinormal modes of Schwarzschild black holes under controlled probes. In the absence of a source, we find that broader initial data suppresses mode excitation, enhancing the relative contribution of the late-time tail, while data localized near the light ring strongly suppresses overtones relative to the fundamental mode. On the other hand, plunging sources give rise to two distinct ringdown stages, associated with scattered radiation generated during the particle&#x27;s infall and with the particle crossing the light ring. Finally, we investigate how much of the signal is sourced from the strong-field region by switching off the source at some finite radius. The resulting waveform is exponentially close -- in cutoff radius -- to that of a standard infall. These results provide insight into the dependence of QNM excitation on the properties and dynamics of the perturbation, and offer a controlled setting in which to investigate the origin of black hole ringdown.</p>

<p>Extreme-mass-ratio inspirals (EMRIs), stellar-mass compact objects spiralling into massive black holes, promise to measure the masses and spins of the central black holes, reveal the strong-field environment around them and gauge cosmic distances via millihertz gravitational-wave observations. <details>
<summary>英文摘要</summary>
<p>Extreme-mass-ratio inspirals (EMRIs) observed by LISA will probe the environments of massive black holes (BHs), including the dark matter (DM) overdensities expected to form around them. We compute the relativistic dynamical friction and accretion experienced by a non-spinning compact object on circular equatorial orbits around a Kerr BH embedded in a collisionless DM distribution, extending previous results in Schwarzschild. The DM is modeled as a spike grown adiabatically from an initial Hernquist profile, and the momentum transfer is obtained by integrating relativistic scattering and capture cross sections over the full, generically anisotropic phase space seen by the secondary. Far from the primary the friction tracks the local DM density, which increases with spin. Near the innermost stable circular orbit of rapidly spinning BHs $(a\gtrsim 0.37)$, however, frame dragging forces bound DM particles to co-rotate, so that the secondary is overtaken by the surrounding medium: the friction weakens and eventually reverses sign where the DM density peaks. We show that a similar reversal can be mimicked around a non-rotating BH if the initial halo carries large net angular momentum, although this requires anisotropies beyond those found in cosmological simulations. We also show that commonly used Chandrasekhar-like prescriptions, which depend only on the local density, can overestimate the friction force. Our results demonstrate that the full phase-space structure of the environment must be included when modeling DM effects on EMRIs.</p>

<summary>英文摘要</summary>
<p>Space-borne gravitational-wave detectors will see tens of millions of Galactic binaries. Resolved, they would form a census of the Galaxy&#x27;s ultracompact binaries unhindered by dust; unresolved, they are the dominant noise up to a few millihertz for massive black-hole binaries, extreme-mass-ratio inspirals and backgrounds from the early Universe. We make it an observable by defining a source as a binary above a signal-to-noise threshold. Neural flows count the binaries to within one in 99% of 1 microhertz intervals over 0.5-10 mHz, and nested sampling resolves them, even pairs closer than one frequency bin. The data themselves set a limit: a source is resolvable only if its signal pays for its own description, about 20 nats, which biases even ideal trans-dimensional samplers. Like the diffraction limit in imaging, this Bayesian confusion limit is set by the data rather than by the method, and it governs any superposition of weak sources, from radio surveys and pulsar timing arrays to super-resolution microscopy. For future space-borne detectors, the method turns the Galactic foreground into a definite, calibrated count of binaries with an analytic selection function, the basis for global fits and for tests of population models.</p>
